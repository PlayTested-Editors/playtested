Supermassive Games has carved out a very specific, entertaining niche for couch co-op horror nights, and *Directive 8020* continues that tradition well. I recently ran through the campaign with two mostly non-gamer friends, splitting the character roster among the three of us. As a gripping, interactive sci-fi movie that you can theorize about on the couch while the plot unfolds, it does its job admirably. However, as an actual video game, it introduces a few clunky mechanics and pacing issues that occasionally drag down the stellar setting.

### **Pros**

* 🛋️ Offers a fantastic suite of accessibility options, including highly customized per-player difficulty settings
* ⏪ Features flexible campaign modes that let you either lock in permanent choices or the ability rewind to explore decision trees
* 🪐 Delivers a genuinely gripping sci-fi horror setting that fuels fantastic couch-theorizing with friends
* 🔀 Boasts high replay value due to the sheer volume of different narrative paths and outcomes

### **Cons**

* 🎮 Rips away player agency during critical moments, forcing characters into unpreventable, stupid decisions via cutscene
* 🚶‍♂️ Suffers from wildly uneven pacing in co-op, occasionally making you wait 20 minutes just to walk like five feet
* 🥷 Introduces full-control manual stealth sections that are incredibly clunky and aggressively unfriendly to casual players

For anyone completely new to Supermassive's formula, the game is essentially an interactive cinematic horror movie. You take control of a massive ensemble cast—in this case, the crew of the deep space colony ship *Cassiopeia*—and navigate them through a terrifying, high-stakes narrative. The core gameplay loop revolves around making split-second dialogue choices, exploring atmospheric environments for clues, and surviving frantic quick-time events (QTEs) when the action suddenly kicks off. Every choice you make actively ripples through the branching story, potentially saving a character's life or dooming them to a gruesome death.

This specific entry trades the traditional haunted houses and teen slashers for a sprawling, terrifying sci-fi setting. The crew is stranded lightyears from Earth near the exoplanet Tau Ceti f, dealing with a mysterious threat. It leans heavily into the claustrophobic, isolated dread and paranoia of classic sci-fi horror films like *The Thing* or *Aliens*. It’s a brilliant backdrop that naturally builds suspicion, making you second-guess whether you can even trust the very crewmates you're supposed to be cooperating with.

When it comes to the actual scares, the game strikes a genuinely impressive balance. Supermassive could have easily taken the lazy route and bombarded the player with cheap jumpscares given the dark, claustrophobic spaceship setting. While there are definitely a few sudden loud noises and jumpy moments designed to make your couch group spill their drinks, the game doesn't overly rely on them. Instead, it builds its terror on creeping, psychological paranoia. The sheer dread of feeling that someone or something is stalking the characters, combined with the heavy consequence of permadeath, generates a suffocating tension that feels much more earned than a basic monster simply popping out of a vent.

### **A Masterclass in Couch Co-Op Accessibility**

Where *Directive 8020* truly shines is in how much control it gives you over the actual campaign structure. Supermassive clearly understands that these games are often played in mixed groups of seasoned players and complete beginners. Being able to tweak difficulty options for each individual player is a good QoL feature. I could set my own quick-time events (QTEs) to be a bit more demanding, while my non-gamer friends had much more forgiving windows so they didn't feel stressed out.

The campaign customization is also incredibly generous. You can assign specific characters to specific players right from the start, and you have total control over the permanency of your choices. If you want a hardcore, high-stakes run, you can lock in permadeath. But if you just want a chill night of exploring the narrative, there is a mode that lowers the stakes entirely, letting you revert deaths and actively explore the different branches of the decision tree without punishing you for a missed button prompt.

### **Passing the Controller Around**

The actual gameplay elements outside of dialogue choices are a bit of a mixed bag. For casual players, the simple exploration gameplay mechanics are perfectly accessible, but seasoned gamers will likely find the actual moment-to-moment gameplay a bit lame and shallow. The graphics are decent and exactly what you expect from a modern Supermassive entry, but they aren't groundbreaking.

The real issue arises in how the game distributes its "roam around" gameplay. Sometimes, you get a lengthy sequence involving actual puzzle-solving and exploration. Other times, the roaming sections are downright insulting. Because we split the characters three ways, there were multiple instances where a friend would finally get their turn with the controller, only to literally walk their character few feet up to an objective, immediately triggering a cutscene that ended their turn. It really sucks the momentum out of a co-op session when someone's entire playable chapter boils down to a probably a 30 second walking animation.

### **Stealth Mechanics Kind of Ruins The Experience**

By far the most annoying addition to the gameplay loop is the manual stealth. Instead of relying on the classic, tension-building QTE heartbeat minigames from their older titles, the developers decided to give players full character control during stealth sections. It is incredibly clunky. Worse, it is notoriously anti-casual. My non-gamer friends struggled with these full-blown stealth mechanics, and honestly, the game would have been much better off just utilizing QTEs for these hiding sequences, or maybe QTE option for the lower difficulties and giving full control on the higher difficulties, or a separate toggle altogether. Perhaps in the next game.

The game also struggles a bit with character intelligence. The plot and twists are generally decent, and the sci-fi setting is a fantastic backdrop for a horror story. But there are several infuriating moments where the characters are just remarkably stupid. As a player, you are sitting there waiting to be handed control so you can make the logical, correct decision to save a situation, but the game simply refuses to give you a dialogue prompt. Instead, it just continues to a cutscene where the character acts on their own and does something completely idiotic. It makes you feel like a spectator in moments where you should be the director or the player.

### **Conclusion**

Despite a few gripes with the clunky stealth mechanics and the occasional illusion of choice, *Directive 8020* is a great time if you have the right crowd. The sci-fi setting is incredibly atmospheric, and the branching narrative paths give it plenty of replayability. If you want a highly accessible horror game to play over a weekend where you can just pass the controller around, drink some beers, and yell at your friends for getting their characters killed, this absolutely hits the mark.
